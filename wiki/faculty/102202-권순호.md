> 권순호 교수는 POSTECH 증강형 재료 설계 연구실(Augmented Materials Design Lab)을 이끄는 책임교수로, 전산재료과학을 통해 지속가능 사회로의 전환에 기여하는 연구를 수행하고 있습니다. 밀도범함수이론(DFT), 분자동역학(MD), 다중 스케일 시뮬레이션과 더불어 전극 전위를 직접 반영할 수 있는 그랜드 캐노니컬 DFT 기법을 활용해 원자 수준에서 반응 메커니즘과 물성의 기원을 규명하고, 이를 실험적으로 구현 가능한 소재 설계 전략으로 확장하는 데 주력합니다. 주요 응용 분야는 그린 수소 생산, 수소 저장, 이산화탄소 환원, 폐수 처리 등 에너지·환경용 고성능 전기화학 촉매 소재 설계이며, 연구 목적에 맞는 새로운 계산 방법론 개발도 함께 진행합니다. 대표 성과로는 동적 CO 전해 조건에서 CO2 생성을 거쳐 탄산염이 형성되며 구리 촉매가 피독될 수 있음을 이론적으로 규명한 Nature Catalysis 2026 논문의 공동 제1저자 연구가 있으며, 이 밖에도 CO2 환원, 산소 발생 반응, 수소 발생 반응 관련 논문을 다수 공동 저술했습니다. 교육 측면에서는 학부 과목 '재료의 기계적 성질'과 대학원 과목 '특론: 불균일 촉매 반응론'을 담당하고 있고, 석·박사과정생과 학부 연구인턴을 모집하고 있습니다.

Welcome to Kwon's group!
The Augmented Materials Design Laboratory develops advanced materials for sustainable energy and environmental technologies by leveraging atomistic simulations to uncover fundamental mechanisms and transform them into experimentally viable design strategies. We employ density functional theory (DFT), molecular dynamics (MD), and multiscale simulation approaches to rigorously elucidate reaction mechanisms and the origins of material properties at the atomic level. Based on these insights, our research focuses on the design of high-performance electrochemical catalyst materials for energy and environmental applications, including green hydrogen production, hydrogen storage, carbon dioxide reduction, and wastewater treatment. By bridging multiple spatial and temporal scales, we seek to achieve a deep understanding of atomic and molecular interactions under realistic reaction environments and to establish practical, reality-oriented materials design strategies that can be directly implemented in experiments. Such computation-driven design serves as a key tool for reducing trial-and-error processes and significantly accelerating the discovery and optimization of novel materials.
증강형 재료 설계 연구실은 원자단위 시뮬레이션을 통해 재료의 본질을 이해하고, 그 지식을 실험적으로 구현 가능한 설계 원리로 확장함으로써 지속가능 에너지 및 환경 기술을 선도하는 신소재를 개발합니다. 본 연구실은 범밀도 밀도범함수이론(DFT), 분자동역학(MD), 다중 스케일 시뮬레이션 기법을 활용하여 원자 수준에서의 반응 메커니즘과 물성의 기원을 정밀하게 규명하고, 이를 바탕으로 그린 수소 생산, 수소 저장, 이산화탄소 환원, 폐수 처리 등 에너지·환경 분야에 요구되는 고성능 전기화학 촉매 소재를 설계·제안합니다. 특히 서로 다른 공간 및 시간 스케일을 아우르는 시뮬레이션 접근을 통해, 실제 반응 환경에서 원자와 분자가 어떻게 상호작용하는지를 깊이 있게 이해하고, 실험적으로 구현 가능한 현실 지향적 소재 디자인 전략을 구축하는 데 주력합니다. 이러한 계산 기반 설계는 시행착오를 줄이고, 신소재 개발의 속도와 효율을 획기적으로 향상시킬 수 있습니다.

Reaction Mechanism and Kinetics
To develop high-performance catalytic materials, it is essential to understand reaction mechanisms occurring on catalyst surfaces. Using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we can quantify the energetic requirements of chemical reactions, including reaction energies and activation barriers. From this information, we can further estimate reaction rates and kinetics.
These energetic and kinetic properties strongly depend on the nature of the catalyst and the surrounding reaction environment. By virtually tuning the identity of catalytic materials or modifying reaction conditions, we can systematically screen a wide range of candidate materials and identify promising catalysts that enhance the desired chemical reactions.
Grand canonical DFT calculations
Conventional density functional theory (DFT) calculations are typically limited to charge-neutral systems. In contrast, electrochemical reactions occur under an applied bias, where the number of electrons in the system continuously varies. As a result, standard DFT approaches cannot directly capture the effect of electrode potential on reaction energetics and kinetics. Grand canonical DFT (GC-DFT) overcomes this limitation by allowing the number of electrons to change at a fixed electrochemical potential, thereby accurately describing the charging behavior of electrochemical interfaces. This framework enables direct and quantitative comparisons between computed reaction energetics and kinetics and experimental electrochemical measurements. In our research, we employ grand canonical DFT to investigate potential-dependent reaction mechanisms and catalytic activity at electrified interfaces, providing realistic atomic-level insights into electrochemical processes under operating conditions.
Multiscale Dynamics
Multiscale molecular dynamics simulations enable us to track atomic trajectories in complex systems across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. Depending on the length and time scales of interest, as well as the specific goals of the simulation, we employ different computational approaches. Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) provides quantum-mechanical accuracy for the entire system, naturally allowing for the formation and cleavage of chemical bonds. However, AIMD is typically limited to systems containing a few hundred atoms and simulation times of less than 1 ns. In contrast, classical force-field-based molecular dynamics (MD) can be applied to systems comprising millions of atoms, reaching timescales up to microseconds. Through classical MD simulations, we can efficiently evaluate equilibrium structures of complex systems as well as mass transport properties, such as diffusion and ionic conductivity, under realistic conditions.

Method Development
Cyclic Voltammetry Simulation
Cyclic voltammetry (CV) is one of the most fundamental experimental techniques in electrochemistry, providing a direct current response to an applied potential. Despite its apparent simplicity, CV contains rich and invaluable information about electrochemical systems. In our research, we simulate CV curves using a bottom-up, atomistic approach based on the energetics of redox reactions occurring at electrode surfaces. This framework enables us to elucidate how surface intermediates evolve as a function of the applied potential. By directly comparing simulated CVs with experimental measurements, we can rigorously validate our theoretical models and gain mechanistic insight into electrochemical processes.
Hybrid QM/MM scheme
Hybrid Quantum Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics (QM/MM) methods are well established in the field of biomolecular simulations. This approach partitions the entire system into two subsystems: a QM region, where a quantum-mechanical description is required (e.g., ligand binding sites in proteins), and an MM region, where chemical reactions do not occur and classical force fields are sufficient. In electrocatalytic systems, however, proton (H⁺) diffusion is strongly coupled with the solvent environment, involving frequent formation and cleavage of chemical bonds with surrounding water molecules. Such processes inherently require a QM-level description. This coupling complicates the application of conventional QM/MM methods, as the boundary between the QM and MM regions is neither fixed nor clearly defined, but instead changes dynamically due to proton hopping. To overcome this limitation, we have developed an adaptive boundary-shifting QM/MM method that enables real-time simulation of proton (and hydroxide) diffusion and their recombination in aqueous environments, where the solvation shells of H⁺ and OH⁻ continuously evolve. This approach naturally captures dynamic proton transfer processes while maintaining computational efficiency. Furthermore, the method can be extended to investigate other mass transport phenomena, such as lithium-ion diffusion in polymer electrolytes (e.g., Nafion).

Applications
Water Electrolysis
Green hydrogen is hydrogen gas produced by directly splitting the H–O bonds of water molecules using sustainable energy sources such as photovoltaic cells or wind turbines. However, in addition to the high cost of renewable electricity and the limited scale of current production, the reliance on expensive catalysts, such as iridium, makes it challenging to achieve the ultimate cost target of $1 per kg of H
2
. In our research, we have investigated the gold-standard IrO
2
catalyst through atomistic modeling to uncover its activity and stability at the atomic level. Based on these insights, we explore strategies to reduce iridium loading or partially substitute iridium with ruthenium, which is approximately ten times less expensive, while maintaining high catalytic performance.
Fuel Cell
Efficient utilization of hydrogen is a key requirement for building a sustainable energy society. In fuel cells, hydrogen is electrochemically converted into electricity and water, offering a clean and highly efficient energy conversion pathway. However, the performance and durability of fuel cells are strongly limited by sluggish electrode reactions at the anode and the use of costly platinum-based catalysts. Our research focuses on understanding electrode reaction mechanisms and we aim to design high-performance and cost-effective catalysts that enable efficient hydrogen utilization with reduced precious-metal content.
CO
2
Conversion
Electrochemical CO
2
conversion provides a promising route to transform carbon dioxide into value-added chemicals and fuels, such as carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons, and alcohols, using renewable electricity. Despite its potential, CO
2
reduction suffers from limited energy efficiency and low selectivity due to competing side reactions. Using first-principles calculations and molecular simulations, we investigate reaction pathways and catalyst–electrolyte interactions on catalytic surfaces. Our goal is to establish atomic-level design principles that enable selective and efficient CO
2
conversion, contributing to carbon-neutral energy cycles.

The Augmented Materials Design Laboratory at POSTECH (Principal Investigator: Prof. Soonho Kwon, [이메일]) is seeking graduate students (M.S./Ph.D.) and interns to join our team. Our research focuses on advancing the transition toward a sustainable society through computational materials science. We aim to understand complex systems from the atomic scale using simulations and to design novel materials with enhanced functionality. Our work spans a wide range of theoretical and computational approaches, from quantum-mechanical modeling of chemical reactions to large-scale simulations based on classical mechanics, as well as the development of new methodologies tailored to specific research challenges. We welcome applications from prospective M.S. and Ph.D. students, as well as undergraduate research interns. Students interested in our research, graduate programs, or individual consultations are encouraged to contact us at any time.
